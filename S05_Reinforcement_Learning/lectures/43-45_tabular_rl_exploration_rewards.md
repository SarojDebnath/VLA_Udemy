## 1. Value and policy iteration (notebook 43)
- **Value iteration:** $V \leftarrow \max_a [r + \gamma P V]$ until the change is $< 10^{-8}$, then act greedily.
- **Policy iteration:** solve $V^\pi$ exactly, improve $\pi \leftarrow \text{greedy}(V^\pi)$, repeat. It converges in a handful of iterations (7) because each step is a big jump.
- Both give the same $V^*$, verified in the notebook.
- **Slippery FrozenLake:** the intended move happens only 1/3 of the time. The *best possible* policy succeeds only about 74–76% of the time. Optimal ≠ always successful: stochasticity sets a ceiling. The optimal policy often moves *away* from holes rather than toward the goal.
- **γ matters:** with γ = 0.5 the agent barely values a reward 10 steps away and the policy degrades; γ → 1 makes it patient.

## 2. Q-learning (notebook 44)
Model-free, off-policy temporal-difference learning:
$$Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\big[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\big]$$
- Learns $Q^*$ from samples generated by *any* exploratory policy (off-policy).
- Converges to $Q^*$ if every (s, a) is visited infinitely often with a decaying α (Watkins & Dayan 1992).
- In the deterministic grid its greedy policy succeeds 100%; it disagrees with value iteration only where actions tie.

## 3. Exploration
Measured greedy success on slippery 4×4 (optimum 0.74):
| Schedule | Result |
|---|---|
| ε decaying 1 → 0.01 | 0.52, 0.75, 0.74: one seed decayed ε before learning enough |
| constant ε = 0.1 | 0.75 on all seeds |
| ε = 0, deterministic argmax | **0.00**: never discovers the goal |
| ε = 0 + optimistic init (Q₀ = 1) | ≈ 0.75: optimism drives systematic exploration |

Subtle point from the 8×8 grid: ε-decay first reaches the goal at episode 43; ε = 0 with *random tie-breaking* still gets there (episode 1240), because ties in an all-zero Q-table act as exploration; ε = 0 with argmax tie-breaking never does. Implementation details become exploration strategies.

**Robotics view:** random exploration in a 7-DoF arm almost never stumbles onto a successful grasp. That's why robot RL relies on demonstrations (S06), shaped rewards, or curricula, and why VLAs start from imitation.

## 4. Reward design (notebook 45)
The agent optimizes **what you pay for**, not what you want.
| Reward | Greedy true success | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| +1 at goal | 0.74 | correct but slow to learn |
| −0.1 × distance every step | 0.04 | **reward hacking:** falling into a hole *ends* the stream of penalties, so the agent learns to die quickly (episode length ≈ 5 steps) |
| +0.1 when near the goal | 0.75 here | can induce hovering; outcome depends on details |
| potential-based shaping $F = \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ | 0.72 | **provably preserves the optimal policy** (Ng, Harada, Russell 1999); in our runs it needed ~20k episodes vs ~6k for the plain reward, so it changes learning speed, here for the worse |

Why potential-based shaping is safe: along any trajectory the shaping terms telescope, $\sum_t \gamma^t F_t = \gamma^T\Phi(s_T) - \Phi(s_0)$, so they can't change which policy is optimal. It's equivalent to initializing Q with Φ (Wiewiora 2003), which can speed up *or* slow down learning.

**Real reward-hacking examples:** a grasping policy that pushes the object toward the camera to look "lifted"; a locomotion policy that exploits simulator contact glitches; an RL-fine-tuned VLA that satisfies a success detector without finishing the task (S16).

## Key takeaways
- Dynamic programming needs the model; Q-learning needs only samples.
- Without exploration, nothing is learned; optimism is a cheap form of exploration.
- Shaped rewards get hacked; use potential-based shaping or sparse success plus demonstrations.
